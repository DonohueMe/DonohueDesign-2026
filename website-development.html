Why Squarespace, Wix, WordPress, or Shopify aren’t a shortcut

I get asked about these constantly, and for good reason — they’re everywhere, they’re cheap, and they promise a site in an afternoon:

  • Squarespace — clean templates, easy to use, built for people who want a fast setup
  • Wix — drag-and-drop simplicity, good for hobby sites and quick launches
  • WordPress — flexible and widely used, but it demands ongoing maintenance most owners don’t have time for
  • Shopify — solid for basic online stores, but generic out of the box

Every one of these gets you a website. None of them ask who your customer is, what they’re searching for, or why they’d pick you over the business three listings down. That’s not a knock on the tools — it’s just not what they’re built to do. A template gives you a page. It doesn’t give you a plan. I build the plan first, then the pages, whether that means a fully custom build or improving a site you already have on one of these platforms.

And the build doesn’t stop at launch. A smart website needs ongoing care — security updates, content changes, speed checks. A site left untouched for two years, template or custom, is a site that’s falling behind.

Signs your current site is costing you customers

Here’s what I hear from Santa Rosa business owners almost every week: “I know my site needs work, but I’m not sure what’s wrong.” Usually they can feel it — leads have slowed, the phone rings less — but they can’t point to the problem.

I can. After 30+ years doing this, the red flags are obvious:

  • Your site takes more than three seconds to load on a phone
  • You have to pinch and zoom just to read anything
  • There’s no clear next step on any page — no phone number up top, no contact form
  • Your content still mentions things from 2018, or worse, placeholder text nobody replaced
  • You Google your own business and your site doesn’t show up on the first page

Most sites I audit around Bennett Valley and Coddingtown have three or four of these at once. The real cost isn’t embarrassment — it’s the customers who never call. They searched, landed, got frustrated, and went somewhere else. That quiet loss compounds month after month.

I built design systems used across 90+ applications at Wells Fargo. I know what works at scale, and I know what works for a shop on Mendocino Avenue. The diagnosis is the same either way: if your site isn’t mobile-first, fast-loading, and built to convert, it’s working against you.

How the process works, step by step

People ask me this constantly: “What actually happens after I say yes?” Here’s how it moves from conversation to launch:

  1. Discovery call — we talk about your business and what you need the site to accomplish, not what it should look like
  2. Strategy and sitemap — I map your pages, your calls to action, and how visitors move through the site
  3. Design concepts — you see real layouts, mobile-first, built around how your customers actually use their phones
  4. Content and build — I write or refine your copy, add your images, and build every page by hand — no drag-and-drop shortcuts
  5. Review and revise — you click through the whole site, and I adjust until it works right
  6. Launch and verify — I test every browser and device, connect your analytics, and make sure Google can find you

The whole process usually takes a few weeks — faster with content ready, longer if we’re starting from scratch. Either way, you work with me directly from start to finish. No junior designer. No handoffs.

What happens after your site goes live

Launch day isn’t the finish line. It’s the starting line.

I see this constantly: a business gets a new site, celebrates for a week, then never touches it again. Six months later, they’re wondering why the phone stopped ringing. The real work starts after launch, when the site begins earning its keep.

In the first 30 days, I submit your sitemap to Google, connect analytics, test every form and button across devices, and check how your site shows up in local search across Sonoma County. Most clients near Old Courthouse Square or Oakmont start seeing organic traffic pick up within four to six weeks — but only with active management behind it.

After that, I handle the ongoing care that keeps a site working: security updates, fresh content, and a regular look at what pages people visit, where they drop off, and what makes them call. That’s not guesswork. It’s how a site stays visible instead of going stale.

Why mobile-first development matters here

Most of your visitors are on their phones. Not some — most. Google reports over 60% of all searches now happen on mobile, and for local businesses in Santa Rosa that number runs even higher. Someone walking near Howarth Park looking for a place to eat, or a couple over by Spring Lake searching “pizza near me,” is pulling out a phone, not sitting at a desk.

If your site loads slow or looks broken on a small screen, they leave — and a three-second delay can cut your conversions nearly in half. I build every site starting from the smallest screen: navigation built for thumbs, fast load times on cellular connections, and tap-to-call buttons placed where people actually look.

Google also ranks you based on your mobile site, not your desktop one. If your desktop version looks great but your mobile version is a mess, you’re invisible in search results — no matter how good the site looks on your laptop.
