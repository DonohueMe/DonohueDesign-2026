Brochure Design in Santa Rosa, CA

A brochure does heavier lifting than a single sheet. It folds. It holds more. It sticks around. A tri-fold sitting in your lobby near Old Courthouse Square, tucked into a welcome packet at a Railroad Square hotel, or left on a partner's front desk at the California Welcome Center — that's a quiet salesperson working while you sleep.

Brochures earn their keep when your audience has time to read. A real estate agent walking a buyer through the listing process. A dental office on Mendocino Avenue explaining services to a new patient. A tasting room sending visitors home with something that matches the experience they had at the bar. You need room to breathe — multiple panels, a clear story, one call to action at the end.

I design every brochure from scratch for Santa Rosa and Sonoma County businesses. No templates. No stock layouts with your logo dropped in. Your piece starts with your goals, your audience, and what you need the reader to do after they unfold the last panel.

What to bring to the first call

You don't need a creative brief or a marketing degree. You need clarity on a few basics. I handle the rest.

Most Santa Rosa owners come to me with a rough idea and a deadline — a grand opening near Santa Rosa Plaza, a seasonal push before harvest, new patient materials for a practice off Cleveland Avenue. That's enough. If you want to make the most of the call, gather these:

  • Your goal for the piece — new client inquiries, event attendance, foot traffic to your shop downtown. One clear goal shapes every panel.
  • Your audience — tourists leaving Railroad Square, homeowners in Bennett Valley, other business owners at a Chamber event. Even a rough sketch helps me choose tone and visuals.
  • Your logo and brand colors — send the files. No logo yet? We can talk about that as step one.
  • Any text you want included — headlines, service lists, a short company story. Doesn't need to be polished. I'll tighten it so every word earns its panel.
  • Examples you like — a tri-fold from a competitor, a brochure you picked up at a tasting room on Westside Road. Showing beats telling.

How the design process works

You work with me. No account managers. No handoffs to a junior designer you've never met.

Here's how it moves:

  1. Discovery call — your business, your audience, what this brochure has to accomplish. A tri-fold for a winery tasting room near Fourth Street has a different job than one for a contractor covering all of Sonoma County.
  2. Creative brief — a short doc locking down format, fold type, message priorities, and brand guidelines. You approve. No surprises later.
  3. First concepts — two or three real layout directions with actual typography, color, image placement, and copy across every panel. You react to something concrete, not wireframe sketches.
  4. Revisions — most clients need two rounds. Some need one. I don't pad the process.
  5. Final files — print-ready PDFs with correct bleed, trim marks, fold lines, and color profiles. Digital versions for email or your website if you need them.

Seven to ten business days, start to finish. Rush jobs happen — I can usually compress the timeline if your Railroad Square opening or Courthouse Square event is breathing down your neck.

One thing I won't do is skip the brief. I've watched too many projects go sideways because someone jumped straight into design without agreeing on the message first. That's how you end up with a beautiful brochure that buries the phone number on the back panel nobody reads.

Because you're working one-on-one with someone who spent years building design systems at Apple, Wells Fargo, Deloitte, and Intel Security, you're getting senior-level thinking on every panel. Not pretty colors. Strategy baked in.

Print-ready files, printed right the first time

Here's where most brochure projects fall apart. It looks great on screen, then comes back from the printer washed out, cropped wrong, or folded on the wrong line. A file that looks right on your monitor isn't the same as one ready for press.

I build every file to land at the printer clean — correct CMYK color, bleed and trim marks, images at 300 DPI, fold lines mapped to your chosen format. Every print shop in Santa Rosa and Sonoma County has its own specs, and getting it wrong means paying for a second run. I've worked with vendors across the Bay Area long enough to know where the pitfalls sit.

You also get source files you can reuse for a reprint, a resized version, or an updated panel when your services change next season.

Design choices that actually earn their keep

Most brochures end up in the recycling bin. The ones that survive earn it with smart choices, not flashy tricks. Your brochure has about three seconds before someone decides to unfold it or toss it. Every panel has to pull its weight.

What drives results:

  • Visual hierarchy across panels — cover hooks them, inside panels build the case, back panel closes with one clear next step
  • White space — breathing room on every fold, not wasted space. Crowded tri-folds kill readability fast.
  • One clear call to action — call this number, visit this address on Mendocino Avenue, scan this code. Pick one and repeat it.
  • Color with purpose — two or three colors max, matching your brand instead of competing with your message

I see owners try to fit an entire website onto a tri-fold. A brochure isn't a website. It's a handshake — it opens the door, it doesn't furnish the house. The businesses that get this right around Old Courthouse Square and Historic Railroad Square are the ones whose materials still sit on counters weeks after they printed them.

Fonts matter more than people think, so I stick to two per piece: one for headlines, one for body. Photos matter just as much — a low-resolution image will sink an otherwise solid design, so if you don't have professional shots, I'll help you figure out what works for your market.

Every design choice points back to one question: what do you want the reader to do next? That's the strategy behind every brochure I build. Not decoration. Direction.
