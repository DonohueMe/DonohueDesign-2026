What to bring to the first call

You don't need a script. You don't need perfect slides. A few things ready makes the first call productive:

  • Your core message — one or two sentences on what people should walk away knowing. Not everything. The one thing that matters most.
  • Brand assets — logo files, colors, fonts you already use. Style guide if you have one. If not, no problem.
  • Rough content or an outline — bullet points, a messy Word doc, even voice memos. I'll shape the structure.
  • Examples you like — screenshots, links, PDFs. Showing beats telling.
  • Your deadline and the setting — big screen at an event space in Montgomery Village, Zoom, emailed as a PDF. Each format changes the approach.

Clients almost always come to me with too much content crammed into too many slides. Part of my job is helping you cut. A deck that tries to say everything ends up saying nothing.

I'm the one reading your brand files. I'm the one building your deck. No account managers. The person who understands your strategy is the same person making design decisions. That matters more than clients expect.

Signs your current slides are costing you the room

You know the feeling. Three slides in and half the room is on their phones. That's not a you problem. That's a slide problem.

I've sat through hundreds of presentations across Santa Rosa — investor pitches on Fourth Street, nonprofit board meetings near the McDonald District — and the same mistakes show up every time. Most people don't realize their slides are working against them until someone finally says something. Or worse, nobody says anything. They just don't call back.

Red flags I see most:

  • Walls of text. If your audience is reading, they're not listening. You've lost the room.
  • Mismatched fonts, colors, and image quality. Looks thrown together. People notice, even when they can't name what's off.
  • No visual flow. Slides jump around with no structure. Your audience can't follow the logic.
  • Stock look. Default PowerPoint themes signal "I didn't put much thought into this."
  • Bullet points doing the heavy lifting. Bullets aren't strategy. They're a crutch.

Often the content is solid. The problem is delivery through design. Your ideas deserve better packaging — audiences retain far more when strong ideas are paired with strong visuals. That gap matters when you're asking someone to write a check.

Ask yourself: after your last presentation, did anyone mention the slides? Did anyone share them? If the answer is no, that tells you something.

Bad slides don't just bore people. They cost you credibility. They cost you deals. And fixing them is usually simpler than you'd expect.
