Presentation Design in Santa Rosa, CA

Most people think presentation design means picking a nice image and dropping in bullet points. That's like saying website design is choosing a color scheme. The real work happens before a single slide gets built.

Every project starts with one question: what does this presentation need to do? Not what should it look like. What outcome are you after? A closed deal. A funded project. A room that remembers your message a week later. That answer shapes every decision — structure, visuals, how one slide flows into the next.

How I work through a project:

  • Talk through your goals, your audience, your setting. A pitch to investors near Old Courthouse Square is different from a keynote at a regional conference.
  • Outline the narrative arc. Every strong deck tells a story. It doesn't dump information.
  • Build custom slide layouts around your brand. No stock templates. No clip art.
  • Handle data visualization — turning spreadsheets into charts people can read across a room.
  • Review the draft with you and refine it until the deck does exactly what you need.

The biggest problem I see isn't ugly slides. It's too much text on every screen. Your audience reads ahead, stops listening, and you've lost them.

I bring 30+ years of design experience — senior roles at Apple, Wells Fargo, Deloitte, and Intel Security, plus three decades running Donohue Design for clients across Santa Rosa and the North Bay. No handoffs. No account managers. You work with the person building the deck.

Advanced presentation design rules I build into every deck

The presentations Santa Rosa businesses actually need

Those rules show up in every deck I build — for wineries in Alexander Valley, tech startups in the SOFA Arts District, and nonprofits pitching to county boards. Different rooms. Same standards.

The requests I handle most:

  • Investor and funding pitches — you need money. Slides have to make the case fast: clear numbers, clean layout, a story that builds confidence.
  • Sales presentations — going out to prospects comparing you to someone else. Every slide has to earn its place.
  • Conference and event decks — if you're speaking, slides should support your voice, not compete with it.
  • Internal training and onboarding — not glamorous. Still matters. A messy training deck tells new hires you don't sweat the details.
  • RFP and partnership proposals — government contracts, joint ventures, vendor applications. Structure and polish.

Most people come to me after fighting with layouts for hours. Fonts wrong, spacing off, the whole thing feeling like a school project. That's not intelligence. That's bad tools and no design strategy behind the work.

A presentation isn't just slides. It's a sequence. Each slide has a job — deliver data, create a pause, ask for the close. I think about that flow for every deck, whether it's five slides or fifty.

The process, from brief to final file

  • Discovery call. Your goals, not your fonts. Who's in the room? What do you need them to do after the last slide?
  • Content outline. Send me your raw material — Word doc, bullets, napkin sketch. I organize it into a clear story arc with a beginning, middle, and strong close.
  • Design direction. Two or three sample slides so you can see the visual approach. Typography, palette, image style. You pick what feels right.
  • Full build. Every slide designed from scratch. No templates. Custom layouts, original graphics, clean data visualization.
  • Review and refine. You get the full deck. Most clients send a few notes. Usually one round of revisions. Sometimes two.
  • Final file delivery. PowerPoint, Keynote, Google Slides, PDF. Ready to present.

Five to ten business days, start to finish. Rush jobs handled.

The biggest improvement usually isn't prettier slides. It's cutting half the content so the room stays with you past slide four.

Signs your current slides are costing you the room

You know the feeling. Three slides in and half the room is on their phones. That's not a you problem. That's a slide problem.

I've sat through hundreds of presentations across Santa Rosa — investor pitches on Fourth Street, nonprofit board meetings near the McDonald District — and the same failure modes show up: the room reads instead of listens, the story jumps, the file looks stock. Most people don't notice until nobody calls back.

Often the content is solid. The packaging isn't. That gap matters when you're asking someone to write a check.

Ask yourself: after your last presentation, did anyone mention the slides — or share them? If not, that tells you something. Fixing it is usually simpler than you'd expect.
