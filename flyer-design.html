Flyer Design in Santa Rosa, CA

A flyer is a single sheet built for speed. One side, sometimes two. One message, one action, done. Handed out at First Friday in the SOFA Arts District, posted on a board in Art Alley, stacked on the counter of a Fourth Street café before a weekend brunch rush. Grand opening. Seasonal sale. Concert at Old Courthouse Square. Flyers work when you need volume, visibility, and a deadline that won't wait.

Nobody stops to read six panels at a farmers market in Historic Railroad Square or outside Santa Rosa Plaza on a Saturday afternoon. They glance. They decide in about three seconds. Your flyer either earns that glance or it doesn't — and the difference is almost never decoration. It's structure.

I design every flyer from scratch for Santa Rosa and Sonoma County businesses. No templates. No stock layouts with your logo dropped in. Your piece starts with one question: what do you need someone to do before they walk past?

Where flyers do their best work downtown and across the county:

  • Street handouts at community events around Old Courthouse Square and Depot Park
  • First Friday art walks and open studios in the SOFA Arts District
  • Restaurant and retail promos along Fourth Street and Mendocino Avenue
  • Farmers markets and seasonal fairs in Historic Railroad Square
  • Grand openings, wine releases, and weekend events across Sonoma County

Need something that sits on a counter for weeks and explains multiple services? That's brochure territory — a different format with a different job. If you're not sure which fits, I'll sort it out in the first conversation.

What to bring to the first call

You don't need a creative brief or a marketing degree. You need clarity on a few basics. I handle the rest.

Most Santa Rosa owners come to me with a rough idea and a hard deadline — a show at the 6th Street Playhouse next month, a harvest event before crush season, a grand opening two blocks from the SMART station in Railroad Square. That's enough. If you want to make the most of the call, gather these:

  • Your goal for the piece — sell tickets, drive foot traffic, fill appointment slots. One clear goal shapes every inch of the layout.
  • Your audience — downtown shoppers, wine tourists, families at a Courthouse Square event. Even a rough sketch helps me choose tone and visuals.
  • Your logo and brand colors — send the files. No logo yet? We can talk about that as step one.
  • Any text you want included — headline, date, offer, address. Doesn't need to be polished. I'll tighten it so every word earns its keep before someone walks past.
  • Examples you like — a flyer from a Mendocino Avenue wine bar, a handout you grabbed at a SOFA gallery opening. Showing beats telling.

Five things. Most owners already have three on their phone.

What you don't need to worry about: paper size, bleed, file formats, print shop specs. Between 30 years in design and running Donohue Design, those calls are second nature. You focus on the message. I focus on making it land before your event date.

Clients apologize all the time for not having "enough" ready. You're not behind. A ten-minute call fills the gaps. And because you work with me directly — not an account manager — nothing gets lost in translation.

How the design process works

You work with me. No account managers. No handoffs to a junior designer you've never met.

Here's how it moves:

  1. Discovery call — your business, your audience, what this flyer has to accomplish before the event. A grand opening flyer for a shop near Santa Rosa Plaza has a different job than a concert promo for a venue in the SOFA Arts District.
  2. Creative brief — a short doc locking down size, message priorities, and brand guidelines. You approve. No surprises later.
  3. First concepts — two or three real layout directions with actual typography, color, image placement, and copy. You react to something concrete, not sketches.
  4. Revisions — most clients need one or two rounds. I don't pad the process, especially when your print deadline is fixed.
  5. Final files — print-ready PDFs with correct bleed, trim marks, and color profiles. Digital versions for email blast or social if you need them.

Five to seven business days for most flyer projects. Rush jobs happen — I can usually compress the timeline when your Courthouse Square event or Railroad Square opening is a week out.

One thing I won't do is skip the brief. I've watched too many projects go sideways because someone jumped straight into design without agreeing on the message first. That's how you end up with a beautiful flyer that buries the phone number in paragraph three.

Because you're working one-on-one with someone who spent years building design systems at Wells Fargo and Apple, you're getting senior-level thinking on every detail. Not pretty colors. Strategy baked in.

Print-ready files, printed right the first time

Here's where most flyer projects fall apart. It looks great on screen, then comes back from the printer washed out, cropped wrong, or with a border nobody asked for. A file that looks right on your monitor isn't the same as one ready for press.

I build every file to land at the printer clean — correct CMYK color, bleed and trim marks, images at 300 DPI, sized to your exact paper stock. Every print shop in Santa Rosa and Sonoma County has its own specs, and getting it wrong means paying for a second run when you needed those handouts yesterday.

You also get source files you can reuse for the next seasonal push, a resized version for social, or a quick reprint when the first stack runs out at your Fourth Street counter.

Design choices that actually earn their keep

Most flyers end up in the trash. The ones that survive earn it with smart choices, not flashy tricks. Your flyer has about three seconds before someone decides to read it or recycle it. Every element on that single page has to pull its weight.

What drives results:

  • Visual hierarchy — headline, offer, call to action, in that order. Nothing fights for attention on a single sheet.
  • White space — breathing room, not wasted space. Crowded flyers kill readability before anyone reaches the date or address.
  • One clear next step — call this number, visit this Mendocino Avenue address, scan this QR code. Pick one and make it impossible to miss.
  • Color with purpose — two or three colors max, matching your brand instead of competing with your message

A restaurant near Fourth Street promoting a new brunch doesn't need six paragraphs — it needs great food photography, the hours, and a QR code to the reservation page. A wine bar handing out promos during a Railroad Square festival needs the offer and the address above the fold. I match the design to how people actually use a flyer: glance, decide, act or walk on.

Fonts matter more than people think, so I stick to two per piece: one for headlines, one for body. Photos matter just as much — a low-resolution image will sink an otherwise solid design, so if you don't have professional shots, I'll help you figure out what works.

Every design choice points back to one question: what do you want the reader to do next? That's the strategy behind every flyer I build. Not decoration. Direction.
