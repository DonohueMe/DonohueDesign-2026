Why wine country seasonality demands more than a site

Your business doesn't run the same way in October as it does in March. Neither should your website.

Santa Rosa sits in the middle of one of the most seasonal economies in California. Harvest season floods the area with visitors. Winter slows things down. Spring picks back up with events and weddings. A restaurant near Railroad Square gets slammed during crush season, then wonders why online reservations drop off by November. A service company in Rincon Valley stays busy year-round, but their site only reflects one version of what they do.

A smart website adjusts to that instead of ignoring it:

  • Rotating hero content that matches your current season or promotion
  • Landing pages built for specific seasonal search terms
  • Automated scheduling that opens or closes booking based on your capacity
  • Lead capture forms that shift based on your busiest service lines

This isn't about redesigning your site four times a year. It's building it once with the flexibility to shift — surfacing your wine dinner series in fall and your private event space in spring, same site, zero extra work on your end.

Google reports that nearly half of all searches have local intent. In a market that swings this hard with the seasons, a site that just sits there looking pretty can't keep up. Your competitors feel the same seasonal pressure. The ones pulling ahead aren't working harder — they're letting their site do the heavy lifting.

Signs your current site is costing you customers

Your website might look fine to you. But "fine" doesn't pay the bills. In 30+ years of design, I've reviewed more websites than I can count, and most owners have no idea how many visitors leave without taking action — they just know the phone isn't ringing like it should.

If any of these sound familiar, your site is quietly losing you customers:

  • It takes more than three seconds to load on a phone.
  • Your contact info is buried or missing near the top of the page.
  • The layout looks cramped or broken on a smartphone screen.
  • You can't find your own business when you search for what you do in your area.
  • There's no clear next step for a visitor — no button, no form, no prompt.

That last one is the real killer. Someone finds you, reads a bit, and then drifts. They leave. They call the next business on the list — not because your work is worse, but because your site didn't give them a reason to stay.

Your website should generate leads and turn browsers into buyers. If it's not doing that, it's not working for you.
